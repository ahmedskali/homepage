I am a first-generation Australian-Italian from an immigrant background. My grandparents and parents migrated to Australia by ship in the mid-1950s and early 1960s. I grew up in Thomastown, Victoria, in the 90s, a blue-collar, lower-income suburb where the neighbours were not just Italian but from elsewhere in the Mediterranean and the Balkans (Macedonia, Greece, Malta, Croatia, Serbia), from Asia (Turkey, Vietnam, China) and from the Middle East (Lebanon). My grandparents worked in labour-intensive industries. My grandfather was a linesman for Telecom. My grandmother started out in textile manufacturing in Melbourne's inner suburbs, West Richmond and South Melbourne, until she lost her hip in a bus accident, took several years to recover, and was left doing what work she could from home. My parents, like most of their generation, had to relinquish their native citizenship and naturalise to get an office job in Australia. Some details of this background are of course mine alone, but for the most part it is the common thread of my community of peers. I am now a migrant in Europe myself, and still passionately engaged in Australian politics, a path a little less common among my peers. Dare I say that this personal exposure to migration, from both ends, brings an informed perspective. It is certainly why I am writing this, and why I am writing it to you rather than about you.

"We came from migrants who had respect and love for this country, who worked hard and were given nothing." This is a narrative I have been hearing a lot, especially in the lead-up to this year's Victorian election, and often from people with surnames like mine who are thinking about voting One Nation for the first time. Yes, no doubt, our parents and grandparents did some major hard yakka and made difficult sacrifices to give us a future with less hardship than theirs; there is no question there. And indeed, I am sure they have a deep appreciation for the opportunity and prosperity that Australia allowed them to achieve. I hear what you're saying, I do. I am not here to tell you that you are wrong about your own family. I am here because that sentence is doing a lot of work in this election, and I would rather we pulled it apart ourselves than leave it to people who have never been to a Thomastown christening. Two things.

1. "We worked hard and were given nothing."

Again, no doubt the work was hard, it likely left a mark on your life, and it is laudable. But a simple question: if something was very difficult, that implies there is room for it to be made less so. After all, don't most of us, immigrant or not, want better opportunities for everyone who comes after us? How is enduring hardship as a rite of passage constructive in any of this? The old "tough love", I guess. Let's come back to that in a moment.

Let's talk about what was given. "Nothing", you say. Here the family memory and the record have parted ways, and it is worth knowing exactly where.

Our grandparents did not wash up on a beach. They were recruited. The Commonwealth created a Department of Immigration in 1945 with a target of growing the population by one percent a year through migration, and it went looking for migrants. The first it found were the Displaced Persons of Europe's camps, among them tens of thousands of Croats, Serbs and Slovenes who would not go home to Tito's Yugoslavia. Australia shipped them over between 1947 and 1952, put them through Bonegilla, and bound them to two years of whatever work the government chose: the Snowy scheme, the railways, the cane fields. Then it signed assisted-passage agreements with Italy in 1951 and Greece in 1952, and from 1952 it paid for the ships through the Intergovernmental Committee for European Migration. Roughly six in ten Greeks who arrived up to 1980 came on those subsidised passages. [check: 119,064 of 206,871, per Neos Kosmos citing ICEM figures] When Belgrade opened its borders in the 1960s, Canberra signed with Yugoslavia too, in 1970, the only communist state it ever did that with. [check]

Italians were a partial exception, and this is where the memory has its kernel of truth. Around four in five Italians came "unassisted", sponsored by a relative or a paesano who paid the fare and vouched for a job and a bed. If your nonno tells you nobody paid his way, he is probably right. But unassisted did not mean unhelped. They arrived into a full-employment economy that the same government was deliberately running hot with public works, Telecom's lines among them. They could use the Commonwealth Employment Service, government hostels if they needed them, free English classes that have run since 1948 and are now the Adult Migrant English Program, and the Commonwealth-funded Good Neighbour Councils that did much of the local settlement work. Their kids went to free state schools and the family drew child endowment from day one. And the sponsorship system itself was not the government standing aside. It was the government outsourcing the cost of settlement to the diaspora because that was cheaper than hostels. Our grandparents were, in the planners' own terms, the cheap way of doing it. "Given nothing" is what it feels like to be the budget option.

There is one place where "given nothing" is literally true, and it is the most useful part of the story. Until the mid-1960s the Social Services Act barred "aliens" from the age and invalid pension outright. [check: s.19 of the 1947 Act; repeal date, I believe 1966] The residence test was twenty years until 1962. A non-British migrant waited five years to naturalise; a Briton waited one, until Whitlam equalised them in 1973. That is why my parents had to hand back their Italian passports for a desk job. So yes, there were things our grandparents were denied. They were denied them for being foreigners, by exactly the kind of politics that is now asking for their grandchildren's vote. We are the people in the room who know how that felt from the inside. That is worth something. It is at least worth a pause before the ballot box.

2. "...Who had respect and love for this country."

The quiet half of this sentence is that today's migrants don't. Before we sign up to that, it is worth asking a question we rarely ask ourselves: did anyone think our grandparents did, at the time?

They were called wogs and dagos. They were "New Australians" who were told to assimilate, to stop speaking Italian or Greek at home, to stop clustering in Carlton and Brunswick and Thomastown and Oakleigh. When the 1952 recession hit, the papers said they were taking Australian jobs, and the migrants who protested at Bonegilla that they had been promised work were reported as rioters. The game they played on a Sunday was, in Johnny Warren's phrase, a sport for "sheilas, wogs and poofters". He made that the title of his memoir because it was what he had heard for fifty years.

Our Croatian and Serbian neighbours had it harder still, and for longer. On Australia Day 1934 a mob in Kalgoorlie burned about eighty homes and businesses belonging to Italians and "Slavs" and two men died. For the next half-century Croats and Serbs were filed under one word, "Yugoslav", on the census, in the newspapers and at the pub, whether they wanted it or not. Croats spent the Cold War under ASIO suspicion as a community. In 1981 six Croatian men in Sydney were jailed for fifteen years over a bomb plot; a judicial inquiry found in 2026 that there was reasonable doubt about at least three of the convictions and pointed to police misconduct, and the case now sits with the Court of Criminal Appeal. [check status before publishing] Serbs got the 1990s: the wars of a country they had left put on their shoulders at school and at work. And when soccer finally wanted to be mainstream, the price was that Melbourne Croatia had to become Melbourne Knights and every club with a flag in its name had to take it down. The Macedonians, meanwhile, woke up in 1994 to find the Commonwealth had decided to call them "Slav-Macedonians" to keep Athens happy. [check] Love for this country was something all of these communities were required to prove, over and over, to people who had already decided.

And here is the part we tell each other but never say out loud. The scepticism went both ways. I heard it all my life, in kitchens and at the club: Australians are careless, they don't eat properly, their houses are dirty, they waste everything, they put their parents in homes. Some of that was fear, and understandably so, and some of it was a way of holding together as a community that had been told it did not belong. But let's not pretend it was morally pure. It was also prejudice, and some of it was racist, and our own kids heard it. I am not raising this to score a point. I am raising it because it is the detail that matters. "Respect and love for this country" did not arrive on the boat with our grandparents. It grew, over decades, in both directions, while both sides said unkind things about each other. It was unrecognised for a long time and acknowledged only in retrospect, once the fruit shop became an institution and the Italian club became part of the furniture. The Vietnamese went through the same arc in the 1980s, when Geoffrey Blainey warned that Australia was taking too many Asians. Lebanese, Sudanese and Indian Australians are somewhere along it now. Love for a country is not something migrants either arrive with or don't. It is something that happens to a community over time, and something the country eventually decides to notice.

We know all this because our own generation turned it into an entire comedic tradition, and we loved it. Wogs Out of Work in 1987. Acropolis Now from 1989. Effie. The Wog Boy. Pizza. Con the Fruiterer, played by Mark Mitchell, an Anglo-Australian, which tells you who was laughing at whom. The entire premise of that comedy was the marginalisation and plight of the immigrant in Australia, the gap between how our parents saw themselves and how the country saw them, and yes, the gap between how they saw Australians and how Australians saw themselves. It worked because every one of us in the audience recognised it. You cannot have grown up on Acropolis Now and now claim with a straight face that our parents were welcomed as patriots. Both memories are ours. Only one of them made it into the slogan.

So, back to tough love. We have arrived at "love and respect for this country". Good. Now that we have, why are we the ones advocating to reproduce the conditions of hardship we had to endure? A rite of passage? Tough love? If that is the argument, then at least be honest about where you started compared with where they will.

Our grandparents walked off the ship as permanent residents, into full employment, with a government that had sent for them, a house they could afford on one factory wage, free English classes, and a pension once they had done their time. Now look at who is actually arriving. Most of the "migration" this election is about is temporary: students, temporary skilled workers, backpackers. They pay full fees and full tax, get no Centrelink, mostly no Medicare, no vote, and no promise they can stay. Permanent skilled migrants wait four years for JobSeeker or Parenting Payment. [check: NARWP, four years for most working-age payments since 2019] The one group whose arrival looks anything like our grandparents' is the refugees who come through the humanitarian program, about twenty thousand a year, who get settlement services and immediate access to payments, and they are, not by coincidence, the ones with the biggest target on their backs.

And then there are the people for whom "given nothing" is not a memory but a description. Those who came by boat between 2012 and 2014 have spent more than a decade on bridging visas, through a "fast track" process that was finally abolished, some with no right to work, some with no Medicare, their income support largely cut from 2018. As of late 2025 more than three thousand were still waiting for a first decision and more than four thousand had no visa at all. [check: Asylum Insight, 30 September 2025] Anyone who has arrived by boat since 2013 is not settled in Australia at all. Say what you like about the policy. Just don't say they were given anything.

If the hardship was real, and it was, the lesson is not that the next lot should cop it too, and cop more of it. The lesson is that we, of all people, know what it costs and what it looks like from the receiving end. That is not a reason for guilt, and it is not a reason to pretend there are no hard questions about housing, services, or how many people a city can absorb in a year. Those are real questions and they deserve real answers, and the people in Thomastown and Lalor and Dandenong are entitled to ask them, because they are the ones competing for the same rentals. But One Nation is selling a cultural answer to a material problem. Fewer Indians will not build a single house in Epping. It is a reason to insist that the answers come from somewhere other than the place our grandparents were on the wrong side of. Pauline Hanson's first speech to Parliament, in 1996, warned that Australia was being "swamped by Asians". Thirty years earlier it was us. The grandchildren of people who were once the problem should at least know what they are signing up for. Know your roots. All of them. Then let's talk.
